# Play Fewer Hands, Price Every Decision

To get past beginner level you have to stop asking "is my hand good?" and start asking three things: **where am I sitting, what price is the pot giving me, and how strong is my hand against this opponent's likely hands on this board?** Your seat decides how many hands you play. Modern 100-big-blind charts open **about 10–16% of hands from the earliest seats, about 25% from the cutoff and 40–55% from the button**, and a winning player folds before the flop "more than 70 percent of the time" ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/texas-holdem-starting-hands-guide/)). The pot size turns each call into arithmetic. You need equity (your share of the pot on average) at least equal to your call divided by the pot after you call, so **a half-pot bet needs 25% and a pot-sized bet needs 33%** ([Poker Skill](https://www.pokerskill.com/charts/odds/)). Pot size compared with the stacks behind it (the stack-to-pot ratio, SPR) tells you whether one pair is worth your whole stack. Usually it is in a re-raised pot, where SPR is about 4. Usually it is not in a normal raised pot, where SPR is about 15–18. After the flop, the board matters more than your two cards. Dry, high boards favour the player who raised before the flop and suit small, frequent bets. Low, connected or one-suit boards shrink overpairs and top pair into medium hands that often want to check. At the low stakes where beginners play, opponents call too much and bluff big river bets less often than theory says. So most of your profit comes from **tight preflop play, betting good hands for value, and folding to big aggression from passive players**, not from bluffing or memorised solver output. Against weak opponents, exploiting their mistakes is "almost always more profitable" than playing a balanced theoretical strategy ([Card Player](https://www.cardplayer.com/online-poker/how-to-play-gto-poker)). Learn the skills in order: preflop charts, then pot maths, then postflop play and hand reading, then adjusting to opponents. Back that with a bankroll of 30–50 buy-ins and a plan for tilt, which one long-time micro-stakes coach calls "the biggest obstacle for newer players" ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/9-key-beginner-mistakes-low-stakes-how-to-fix-22957.htm)).

## Your seat decides how many hands you can play

Position means your seat relative to the dealer button, and it fixes the order of action for the whole hand. After the flop the button always acts last and the blinds always act first. Acting last pays because "the player in position always enjoys an information edge": you see what your opponent does before you decide. It also gives you control of the pot size, since you can check behind to end a betting round or raise when someone bets into you ([Red Chip Poker](https://redchippoker.com/position-preflop-postflop/)). Early seats also have more players left to act behind them, any of whom may raise, so their ranges must be tighter ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/preflop-open-strategy-rfi-explained/)). The blinds show what bad position costs. Even strong players are estimated to lose **about 15 big blinds per 100 hands from the small blind and about 30bb/100 from the big blind**. Solver strategies open only **9.3% of hands under the gun but 40.4% from the small blind** (100bb stacks, 4x opens). Position is worth more when stacks are deep. When stacks are short compared with the pot, its value can be "completely eradicated," because both players are committed on most flops ([Red Chip Poker](https://redchippoker.com/position-preflop-postflop/)).

Chart labels differ between sources, so match each chart's names to your seat. In 6-max, the first three full-ring seats disappear. The 6-max "UTG" seat (PokerCoaching calls it "LJ") plays roughly like the full-ring lojack ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/preflop-charts/); [Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/preflop-open-strategy-rfi-explained/); [Red Chip Poker](https://redchippoker.com/what-to-open-raise-preflop/)).

| Seat group | 9-handed (full ring) | 6-max |
|---|---|---|
| Early | UTG, UTG+1, UTG+2 | none |
| Middle | Lojack (LJ, sometimes "MP"), Hijack (HJ) | UTG/LJ (plays like full-ring LJ), HJ (sometimes "MP") |
| Late | Cutoff (CO), Button (BTN) | CO, BTN |
| Blinds | Small blind (SB), Big blind (BB) | SB, BB |

Full ring needs tighter early play. PokerCoaching's 8-handed cash charts open **11.4% under the gun, 15.7% from the lojack, 26.1% from the cutoff and 40.3% from the button** ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/preflop-charts/)). Upswing's simpler 9-handed beginner list opens only 77+, ATs+, KTs+, QTs+, T9s and AQo+ under the gun ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/texas-holdem-starting-hands-guide/)). Older, live-oriented advice from Red Chip goes tighter still: 77+, AQs+ and AKo ([Red Chip Poker](https://redchippoker.com/what-to-open-raise-preflop/)). That is 68 of 1,326 possible starting combinations, about 5%, or a third to a half of the solver ranges. A beginner at a loose live table can sensibly lean toward the tighter lists. The charts agree on structure even where the percentages differ. Pairs and suited aces come in early. Suited kings, queens and connectors widen steadily toward the button. Offsuit hands come in last, big cards first: AJo and KQo early, then ATo, KJo and QJo, then weak offsuit aces and kings only on the button. PokerCoaching suggests memorising the cut-off for each category (for example, "suited kings down to K9s") instead of every cell, and studying charts between sessions, because many sites ban using them while you play ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/preflop-charts/)). Upswing also advises dropping the bottom of each range when loose players sit behind you or in the blinds ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/preflop-open-strategy-rfi-explained/)).

### Pairs, suited hands and offsuit high cards win in different ways

Knowing *why* a hand is in a chart tells you how to play it after the flop. You are dealt a pocket pair only **5.88% of the time**. It flops a set (three of a kind using both your cards) **11.8% of the time, about 7.5-to-1 against**, while an unpaired hand flops a pair about 29% of the time ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/odds-of-flopping-poker-hands/)). Small pairs therefore make money mainly by "set mining": calling cheaply, hoping to hit a set and win a big pot. That only works when enough money is left in the stacks, as the pot-odds section explains. Suited cards flop a made flush less than 1% of the time, and connectors flop a straight only about 1.3% of the time. Their value comes from draws and from strong hands that are hard to see coming. As PokerCoaching puts it, "suitedness alone is not a big factor in hand strength" ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/odds-of-flopping-poker-hands/)). Offsuit high-card hands such as KJo and ATo have the opposite problem: domination. When KJo pairs its king, it loses big pots to KQ and AK, which are exactly the hands tight players raise and re-raise with. When it is ahead, it usually wins only a small pot. That is why Jonathan Little advises folding dominated offsuit hands like KJ and AT when someone re-raises your open, while calling with suited hands and pairs that flop well ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/3-bet-poker-strategy/)). It is also why defending the big blind with J9o is worse than with 98s. 98s has slightly less raw equity but uses it better, because it makes fewer second-best hands and more strong draws ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/mastering-big-blind-strategy-a-guide-to-profitable-defence/)). In Upswing's categories, **TT+ and AK/AQ are raised from every seat**. Middle pairs are good but should be played less aggressively. Pairs 22–44 are not raised from early seats and become profitable later, mainly as set-mining hands. Hands like Q5o or 84o are never opened, because "Don't get bored and start opening with trashy hands!" ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/texas-holdem-starting-hands-guide/)).

### Raise when you are first in, and size the raise to the table

The single most important preflop rule is to **open-raise rather than open-limp** (just calling the big blind when nobody has entered). Upswing says to "always raise first in rather than limp" ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/preflop-open-strategy-rfi-explained/)). Raising builds a pot with your playable hands, cuts the number of opponents, gives you the initiative to bet the flop, and stops you getting raised off your hand ([BlackRain79](https://www.blackrain79.com/2024/05/preflop-poker.html)). Online, open to **2–2.5bb**. Upswing uses a flat 2.25bb, or 2bb with 2.5bb from the button, and PokerCoaching uses 2.5bb with 3bb from the small blind ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/preflop-open-strategy-rfi-explained/); [PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/preflop-charts/)). Live, about 3bb is standard. Red Chip goes to 4–5bb against loose players who call too much, and adds 1bb for each player who limped in front of you ([Red Chip Poker](https://redchippoker.com/what-to-open-raise-preflop/)).

When someone has already raised, you have three choices: re-raise (3-bet), call or fold. Little's framework re-raises for value with **QQ+ and AK, adding JJ and AQs against late-position openers**. It re-raises as a bluff with small suited aces (A5s–A2s) from the button or small blind, because they block the opener's strong aces. It calls with suited connectors, medium pairs and big cards that play well in position. Against early-position openers it 3-bets very little: in the 100bb cash chart, the button re-raises only about 4% of hands against an under-the-gun open. Re-raise to **about 3x the open in position and 4x out of position**, adding one extra open-size for each player who has already called. Against players who call everything, 3-bet only for value ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/3-bet-poker-strategy/)). Red Chip adds that inexperienced online 6-max players can simply play **3-bet-or-fold** against raises ([Red Chip Poker](https://redchippoker.com/position-preflop-postflop/)). If your own open is re-raised, Upswing's in-position defaults are: AA–JJ mostly 4-bet, AK 4-bet; TT–66, AJ, KQ, KJ and suited connectors call; 22–55 usually fold. Weak offsuit aces and kings rarely continue, and against a tight player you fold everything except the top of your range ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/vs-3-bet-pre-flop-position-strategy-revealed/)). A 4-bet is about **2.3–2.5x the 3-bet** ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/preflop-charts/)).

### Defend the big blind widely, and play the small blind raise-or-fold

The big blind has already paid one blind, so it gets a good price. Against a 2.5bb open with no antes, you call 1.5bb to win a final pot of 5.5bb, so you need only **about 27% equity** (22% against a 2bb open). That price is why the big blind defends hands it would never open. PokerCoaching's 2026 guidance is to call with suited connectors, suited aces and suited broadways (two suited cards ten or higher). It also says to 3-bet premiums plus a few "blocker" bluffs (hands holding a card the opener needs for their strongest hands), and to fold dominated offsuit hands like J9o. After the flop, don't fold too easily to small bets, and be more aggressive on low, connected or paired boards, which suit the big blind's range ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/mastering-big-blind-strategy-a-guide-to-profitable-defence/)). The small blind is the hardest seat. You are "forced to pay half of a big blind without looking at your cards" and then act first on every street. Upswing's cash-game advice is to open **about 40–50% of hands to about 3bb** when everyone folds to you, and to **3-bet or fold, not call**, when someone has already raised ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/small-blind-poker-strategy-tips/)). Solver-based charts from PokerCoaching and Red Chip do mix in limps and calls from the small blind ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/preflop-charts/); [Red Chip Poker](https://redchippoker.com/position-preflop-postflop/)). That is a refinement a beginner can safely skip.

### Short stacks kill speculative hands, and tournaments get there fast

Small pairs and suited connectors need deep stacks and opponents who will pay you off when you hit. SplitSuit calls set mining against a 25bb stack "pointless." PokerCoaching notes that deeper stacks add hands like 43s to button opens, while shorter stacks favour high-card hands like K7s that win by making top pair ([SplitSuit](https://www.splitsuit.com/small-pocket-pair-strategy); [PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/preflop-charts/)). Tournaments add a second layer. Blinds rise, and antes (small forced bets from every player) put extra money in every pot, which widens opening and shoving ranges. At **about 10–15bb or less, preflop play becomes mostly all-in or fold**. Between about 10 and 20bb you mix shoves with small raises. Above about 20bb, a min-raise usually beats shoving ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/push-fold-tournament-strategy-charts/); [888poker](https://www.888poker.com/magazine/strategy/push-fold-charts)). Near the prize-money places, payout pressure (ICM) tightens everything. At 10bb, Upswing's ICM-adjusted chart shoves only 8.5% of hands from under the gun, while PokerCoaching's standard 10bb chart shoves 16.9%. The gap probably reflects payout pressure plus different ante assumptions ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/push-fold-tournament-strategy-charts/); [PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/preflop-charts/)). These tournament adjustments make most sense after cash-game basics are solid.

## Pot size sets the price of every call and every commitment

Most beginners decide whether to call by feel. Stronger players compare a price with a probability. The rest of this section adds the details you need to do that at the table.

### Divide your call by the pot after you call

The equity you need to call equals **your call divided by the total pot after you call**. If the pot is 100 and your opponent bets 50, you call 50 to win a final pot of 200, so you need 25%. The commonest beginner mistake is comparing the call with the pot *before* the bet (50 against 100 looks like "50%"). The same formula handles raises. If you bet 50 into 100 and get raised to 200, you call 150 into a final pot of 500 and need 30% ([Poker Skill](https://www.pokerskill.com/charts/odds/); [Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/implied-odds-poker-strategy/)). Little advises beginners to rely on pot odds before the more abstract defence-frequency maths covered below ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/mdf-poker/)). The table below is the most useful single reference in this report. The same arithmetic produces four numbers for each bet size. The bluff and defence columns come from GTO Wizard and PokerCoaching. The last column shows how many value bets per bluff a balanced river bet carries ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/mdf-alpha/); [PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/mdf-poker/); [Poker Skill](https://www.pokerskill.com/blog/optimal-bluffing-frequency-formula/)).

| Bet size | Pot odds offered | Caller needs equity | A pure bluff must succeed | Minimum defence frequency | Balanced river value : bluff |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1/4 pot | 5 : 1 | 16.7% | 20.0% | 80.0% | 5 : 1 |
| 1/3 pot | 4 : 1 | 20.0% | 25.0% | 75.0% | 4 : 1 |
| 1/2 pot | 3 : 1 | 25.0% | 33.3% | 66.7% | 3 : 1 |
| 2/3 pot | 2.5 : 1 | 28.6% | 40.0% | 60.0% | 2.5 : 1 |
| 3/4 pot | 2.33 : 1 | 30.0% | 42.9% | 57.1% | 2.33 : 1 |
| Pot | 2 : 1 | 33.3% | 50.0% | 50.0% | 2 : 1 |
| 1.5x pot | 1.67 : 1 | 37.5% | 60.0% | 40.0% | 1.67 : 1 |
| 2x pot | 1.5 : 1 | 40.0% | 66.7% | 33.3% | 1.5 : 1 |

Two patterns are worth memorising. First, **half-pot needs a quarter, pot needs a third, and no bet ever needs 50%**. Even a huge overbet asks for less than half the equity. Second, the balanced value-to-bluff ratio on the river equals the pot odds you offer: a half-pot bet gives 3:1, so it carries three value hands per bluff.

### Count only outs that actually win

An "out" is an unseen card that should make your hand the winner. To estimate your chance of hitting, the Rule of 2 and 4 says to multiply your outs by 2 with one card to come and by 4 with two cards to come. The shortcut works well up to about 8–10 outs and then overstates big draws ([Poker Skill](https://www.pokerskill.com/charts/odds/)). The exact figures below are worked out from the deck and agree with Poker Skill's published chart. For draws above 8 outs, subtracting (outs − 8) from the ×4 figure brings the shortcut within about a point.

| Draw (typical example) | Outs | Hit by river (from flop) | Hit on river (from turn) | Odds against, one card |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gutshot straight draw, or two pair to full house | 4 | 16.5% | 8.7% | 10.5 : 1 |
| Two overcards (often only partly live) | 6 | 24.1% | 13.0% | 6.7 : 1 |
| Open-ended straight draw | 8 | 31.5% | 17.4% | 4.8 : 1 |
| Flush draw | 9 | 35.0% | 19.6% | 4.1 : 1 |
| Flush draw + gutshot, or flush draw + overcard | 12 | 45.0% | 26.1% | 2.8 : 1 |
| Flush draw + open-ended straight draw | 15 | 54.1% | 32.6% | 2.1 : 1 |

The Rule of 4 has a trap. **The two-card figure applies only when someone is all-in**, so you are certain to see both cards for this one price. With stacks still behind, you will probably face another bet on the turn, so compare the one-card odds with the current price. If you face a 50 all-in into 100 on the flop with a flush draw, calling is worth about +20 on average (0.35 × 150 − 0.65 × 50). The same bet on the turn, with one card to come, is worth about −11, a fold unless future winnings make up the gap. Outs also need discounting. Poker Skill's examples: 9♠8♠ on K♠5♠2♣ faces bigger flush draws in the opponent's range, so its 9 raw outs are worth about 6, and the call becomes a fold. T♠9♦ on A♣7♥8♥ loses two of its straight cards, because they also complete a heart flush. Pairing your ace against a set wins nothing ([Poker Skill](https://www.pokerskill.com/blog/counting-outs-clean-vs-dirty/)).

### Implied odds pay only when stacks are deep and your hand is hidden

Implied odds are "the amount of money that you expect to win on later streets if you hit one of your outs." They can justify calls that fail on the current price. Upswing's example: K♥Q♥ facing $15 into $20 on the turn needs 30% but has about 20%. The call is still right if you can win **more than $25 more on the river** when you hit, which is realistic with a nut draw. A J♦7♦ draw out of position, facing a player with only $117 behind, fails the same test, because "we won't have the nuts when we hit" ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/implied-odds-poker-strategy/)). For set mining, the 7.5-to-1 odds against flopping a set would break even only if you won everything every time you hit. In practice you don't always get paid, and sometimes you lose a big pot to a bigger set. Nathan Williams therefore asks for **at least 15 times the raise behind**, and about 25x when calling out of position ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/set-mining-101-do-you-have-the-right-odds-to-hit-your-set-35667.htm); [BlackRain79](https://www.blackrain79.com/2019/12/is-set-mining-profitable.html)). SplitSuit uses 25x as a general guide ([SplitSuit](https://www.splitsuit.com/small-pocket-pair-strategy)). Those multiples apply to the **effective stack**, the smaller of the two stacks involved, because you can never win more than your opponent has ([ThePokerBank](https://thepokerbank.com/strategy/concepts/spr)). With $500 against a player holding $80 who raises to $8, 15 × $8 = $120 is more than $80, so calling with 44 to hit a set fails the test.

The reverse effect, **reverse implied odds**, is "the amount of money you may lose on future streets after calling a bet." It is why second-best draws and weak pairs bleed money. In Upswing's example, J♦5♦ on T♥9♣5♥ has many bad turn cards, and a solver folds it about 73% of the time, while the same pair with 5♦3♦ is a better call. 66 on K♥9♠3♦ has 39% equity against a 30% price and should still fold, because "you will lose the pot every time your opponent bets on the river" ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/reverse-implied-odds/)). Before calling with a draw, ask whether you are sure to be best if you hit. If not, count fewer outs and expect to win less.

### Bluffs and defence follow the same arithmetic

Expected value (EV) is the average result of a decision if you repeated it many times. For a call, EV = (equity × the pot you win) − ((1 − equity) × your call). Choose the option with the highest EV, not the one most likely to win this particular hand. A bluff has its own break-even point. GTO Wizard calls it "alpha," the bet divided by (bet + pot), and EV(bluff) = fold% × pot − call% × bet ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/mdf-alpha/)). A pot-sized bluff must work half the time, while a one-third-pot bluff needs only 25%. The flip side is the minimum defence frequency (MDF): how much of your range you must continue with so your opponent cannot profit by bluffing with any two cards. MDF is a sanity check, not an instruction to call with junk. PokerCoaching warns that "mechanically applying MDF against players who almost never bluff is not GTO; it's a leak," and notes that low-stakes live players bluff "significantly less than GTO requires" ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/mdf-poker/)). GTO Wizard agrees that you should "put down your shield" and fold more against opponents whose bets are mostly strong hands ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/mdf-alpha/)).

### Stack-to-pot ratio decides whether one pair is worth your stack

Divide the effective stack by the pot on the flop and you have the **stack-to-pot ratio (SPR)**, the main way pot size should shape your plan ([Pokerology](https://www.pokerology.com/poker/strategy/spr/)). With 100bb stacks, a button 2.5bb open called by the big blind leaves a 5.5bb pot and 97.5bb behind, **SPR about 17.7**. Re-raise to 11bb and get called, and the pot is 22.5bb with 89bb behind, **SPR about 4**. A called 4-bet to 24bb gives SPR about 1.6. Sources draw the lines in different places, as the table shows. Pokerology calls its bands "a practical map, not a rigid rulebook." The DeucesCracked and PokerTube pages are of uncertain editorial authority.

| Source | One pair or overpair can commit | Middle "decision" zone | Want two pair, sets or nut draws |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Pokerology](https://www.pokerology.com/poker/strategy/spr/) | 0–3 | 3–6 | 6–10; 10+ is pot control |
| [DeucesCracked](https://www.deucescracked.com/blog/poker-spr-stack-to-pot-ratio-strategy-guide-2026) | under 3 | 3–6 | over 8 |
| [PokerTube](https://www.pokertube.com/article/what-is-spr) | 1–3 | 4–6 | 7–12; 13+ implied-odds hands |
| [ThePokerBank](https://thepokerbank.com/strategy/concepts/spr) | 0–6 | 7–16 | 17+ |

The working rule all four support: **below about 3, top pair with a good kicker or better is happy to go all-in. Between 3 and 6, overpairs and strong top pairs can commit on safe boards. Above about 10, one pair is a pot-control hand**, and you want two pair or better before putting in your whole stack. Upswing makes the same point: in single-raised pots "the stack-to-pot ratio is usually too high to bet top pair for three streets of value" ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/when-to-check-top-pair/)). The arithmetic explains why. To get all-in by the river with three equal bets, you need about 54% pot each street at SPR 4, but more than a pot-sized bet each street at SPR 15–18. Only very strong hands want to bet that much. This is also why Upswing recommends small 25–40% continuation bets in 3-bet pots and planning sizes across all streets so you don't leave an awkward tiny river bet ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/bet-size-strategy-tips-rules/)). Before the flop, PokerCoaching applies the same idea to re-raises: if a 3-bet would commit about a third of your stack, shove or fold instead ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/3-bet-poker-strategy/)).

## The board decides what your hand is really worth

After the flop, hand strength is relative. It depends on how your hand compares with the hands your opponent could hold, and how many betting rounds ("streets") of value it can earn on this particular board. Red Chip's rough guide: **top pair with the top kicker or better earns about three streets, top pair or strong second pair about two, and weaker pairs about one**. Board texture and opponent type shift those numbers ([Red Chip Poker](https://redchippoker.com/4-questions-before-c-betting/)). The kicker matters. On A♥T♦9♠ a solver bets 57 of 102 top-pair combinations, and roughly the best five kickers are a practical cut-off ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/when-to-check-top-pair/)). Overpairs swing the most. On 9-8-3 or 8-7-3, with no straight possible yet, an overpair is "effectively the super nuts" and should bet big. Once three straight cards are on board (8-5-4, J-9-7), it can be "nearly dead" against a made straight and should drop to about 60% pot ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/bet-sizing-on-connected-boards-pot-it-or-slow-down/)). Re-sort your hand into one of the buckets below on every street. The table is a synthesis of these sources, not a single published system. For example, K♦J♣ is a strong two-to-three-street hand on K♠7♦2♣. On K♠8♠3♠ it drops to medium, because any spade flush beats it.

| Bucket | Typical holdings | Default plan |
|---|---|---|
| Monsters | Sets, straights, flushes, strong two pair | Build the pot over three streets; slowplay only on dry boards where you block the opponent's continuing hands |
| Strong made hands | Overpairs and top pair, top kicker on static boards | Two to three streets of value |
| Medium hands | Top pair weak kicker, second pair, overpairs on wet boards | One to two streets; often check-call or check back |
| Weak showdown value | Bottom pair, underpairs, ace-high | Check and try to reach a cheap showdown |
| Strong draws | Nut flush draw, open-ended straight draw, combination draws | Semi-bluff (bet a draw that can still improve) or call |
| Weak draws | Gutshots, backdoor draws, overcards | Selective bluffs, usually in position |
| Air | Nothing | Bluff players who fold; otherwise give up |

### Texture decides whose range owns the flop

Board texture means how high, connected, suited and paired the flop is, and it decides whose *range* the flop helps. A player's range is every hand they could plausibly hold given how they have played. GTO Wizard's flop rules: **bet more on high-card flops that keep the preflop raiser's advantage, and on paired and disconnected flops**. Use bigger, less frequent bets on flops with open draws. Bet less and smaller on flops that complete straights or flushes in the caller's range. Monotone (single-suit) flops make "frequency and sizing drop sharply." Ace-high flops are bet *less* often than 8- through K-high ones, because the big blind holds many aces and check-raises more. The tighter your opening seat, the bigger your edge on the flop ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/flop-heuristics-ip-c-betting-in-cash-games/)). The reverse holds on low, connected boards. On 9-8-7, an early-position raiser's best hand is usually an overpair. The big blind's range "includes sets, two pair, straights, and connected hands," so strong players check-raise there a lot ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/how-to-put-your-opponent-on-a-range-step-by-step/)). GTO Wizard separates two ideas. **Range advantage** (more total equity) mainly decides *how often* you bet. **Nut advantage** (more of the very best hands) decides *how big*. A solver bets mostly 33% on Q♥Q♣6♦, mostly 75% plus 125% overbets on K♥J♥7♦, and drops back to small, half-frequency bets on Q♦J♦T♦, where both players hold plenty of nut hands and draws won't fold ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/the-mechanics-of-c-bet-sizing/)).

### Continuation-bet by board, position and number of players

A continuation bet (c-bet) is a flop bet by the player who raised before the flop. Rather than c-betting every flop at one size, modern advice sets both size and frequency by board and situation. Upswing's rules of thumb are **25–35% pot on dry, static boards; 55–80% on wet, dynamic boards; at least 66% when betting the turn again; and 25–40% in 3-bet pots** ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/bet-size-strategy-tips-rules/)). Out of position, the raiser's range edge "usually doesn't offset the informational disadvantage." The exceptions are boards like A72, 442, KK3 and AKJ, where a small, wide c-bet works ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/continuation-bet-c-bet-strategy-position/)). Checking your whole range out of position is "a decent strategy that is simpler to implement" ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/semi-bluff-poker-strategy/)). On a T♠9♥4♣ flop, Upswing's in-position plan is to **bet QT or better for value, check medium hands from 55 to JT and some ace-high, bluff with straight draws and backdoor draws, and give up the worst hands**. Out of position the betting range shrinks noticeably ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/continuation-bet-c-bet-strategy-position/)). One thing solvers changed: Upswing's editors now note that solvers prefer *large* bets on low, disconnected boards like 8-4-2 ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/66-and-100-c-bet-sizes/)). Red Chip gives a quick exploit check. If an opponent folds more than about 40% to a two-thirds-pot bet, c-betting profits automatically. At about 60% folds, c-bet nearly everything ([Red Chip Poker](https://redchippoker.com/4-questions-before-c-betting/)). The table below is a beginner default built from these sources.

| Situation | Default |
|---|---|
| Heads-up, in position, dry high board (K♠7♦2♣, A♥8♣3♦, Q-Q-4) | Bet most hands small (about 25–33%); check some weak showdown hands |
| Heads-up, in position, connected or two-tone middling board (T♠9♥4♣, J♥T♥6♣) | Bet less often at 66–75% with top pair good kicker+, strong draws and good backdoor bluffs; check medium hands |
| Low, connected board that favours the caller (7♠6♠5♦, 9-8-7) | Check a lot; bet strong hands and strong draws, larger and polarised |
| Monotone flop | Bet less often and smaller |
| Paired flop (K♦K♣4♥, 7-7-3) | Bet often and small |
| Out of position as the raiser | Check much more; bet small only on strongly raiser-friendly boards |
| Three or more players | No automatic c-bets; bet strong value and strong draws, check and give up the rest |

### Bet for value or as a bluff, never "to see where you are"

There are two core reasons to bet. **For value, worse hands must call often enough. As a bluff, better hands must fold often enough.** Steve Selbrede lists betting "for information," betting purely "to protect your hand," small "blocking" bets and betting for "balance" against low-stakes live players as weak reasons. A fold tells you nothing, and a call is ambiguous ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/five-weak-reasons-to-bet-and-one-weak-reason-not-to-28125.htm)). Modern theory still counts protection, meaning denying equity to draws, as part of a bet's value. The mistake is betting *only* for that, without asking whether betting beats checking ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/principles-of-turn-strategy/)). A simple test: before betting, name which worse hands call or which better hands fold. If neither group is meaningful, check. GTO Wizard sums up turn play the same way: "Strong hands grow the pot, medium hands check, and weak hands bluff or give up" ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/principles-of-turn-strategy/)). Thin value bets, which win only a bit more than half the time when called, matter most against players who call too much. Upswing's advice is to "value bet much more against calling stations," including hands like 99 and A8s on all three streets. "Never bluff a calling station" is the better version of "never bluff a fish" ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/thin-value-bet-poker-strategy/)).

Good bluffs are hands with little chance of winning at showdown and some chance to improve. That is why semi-bluffs (betting draws) beat pure air: they win when the opponent folds now and when the draw hits later. Solvers c-bet combination draws about 85% of the time in position, open-ended straight draws about 70%, and king- or ace-high flush draws 70–80%. Queen-high and lower flush draws are bet only about 22% ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/semi-bluff-poker-strategy/)). On the river, GTO Wizard's rule is to pick bluffs that **block your opponent's calling hands and don't block their folding hands**. Missed straight draws usually make better river bluffs than missed flush draws, because a missed flush draw removes some of the opponent's own missed flush draws, which would have folded ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/picking-the-right-semi-bluffs/)).

### Position and player count change the plan

In position, you can bet medium hands for one thin street of value or check back for a free showdown, and you can take free cards with draws. Out of position, the same medium hand usually wants to check and call, and check-raises should come mainly from strong hands and big draws ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/semi-bluff-poker-strategy/)). Multiway pots, with three or more players, change the maths more than beginners expect. A bluff now has to get past several players. A half-pot bluff that needs 33% folds heads-up needs **about 58% folds from each of two opponents** ([Poker Skill](https://www.pokerskill.com/blog/optimal-bluffing-frequency-formula/)). GTO Wizard's multiway rules follow from that. "Tight is right," "stop range betting," "nut potential is king," use smaller bet sizes, and "almost never bluff without solid drawing equity." Position gains value too: in one example the button defends 8% wider than the small blind despite a worse price ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/10-tips-multiway-pots-in-poker/)). A 2018 PokerNews article argues for c-betting multiway at soft small-stakes tables anyway ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/small-stakes-poker-plays-3-continuation-betting-multi-way-31928.htm)). Treat that as a table-specific exploit, not a default.

### Read ranges, not single hands

The biggest step in hand reading is to stop putting opponents on one exact hand. "Ranges get smaller as the hand progresses," because each action removes hands that don't fit it ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/hand-reading-poker/)). PokerCoaching's process runs in order. Identify the player type; a player who joins 50% of pots but raises only 3% limps almost everything. Build a preflop range from position and action. Narrow it street by street, since "aggressive bets remove weak holdings; checks and calls often signal medium-strength hands." Check who the board favours, then adjust to how much logic that player's actions carry ([PokerCoaching](https://pokercoaching.com/blog/how-to-put-your-opponent-on-a-range-step-by-step/)). Counting combinations makes this concrete. Each pocket pair has 6 combinations, and each unpaired hand has 16 (4 suited, 12 offsuit). Cards you can see remove combinations, so on a king-high board KK has only 3 left. Upswing's worked hand shows the method. With K♥8♥ in the big blind against a cutoff open, on K♠5♦4♠, the turn call is right: 38.9% equity against the betting range versus 29.8% needed. When the 2♠ river brings a $17 bet into $23.50, the cutoff's betting range is sets, straights, flushes and missed draws. One- and two-pair hands would check. Top pair now has 26.8% equity against a 29.6% price, so it **folds** ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/hand-reading-poker/)). That hand ties the whole method together: position and action set the range, the board and betting narrow it, and pot odds give the final answer.

Here is an illustrative hand that applies the routine end to end. You hold A♠J♠ on the 6-max button at 100bb, and everyone folds to you. It is in the button chart, so you open to 2.5bb, and the big blind calls. The pot is 5.5bb and the SPR is about 18, so one pair is not yet a hand you want to go all-in with. The flop is J♦7♣2♥, a dry, jack-high board that favours your raising range. Top pair, top kicker is in the "strong made hand" bucket, so you bet small, about a third of the pot. If called, you bet again on a safe turn at two-thirds pot or more, following Upswing's rule for turn barrels. On the river, given the high SPR, you decide between a third value bet and checking based on the river card and the opponent. A large check-raise from a passive player is a fold more often than your instincts suggest.

## Low-stakes opponents call too much and bluff too little

Game-theory-optimal (GTO) play is a balanced, unexploitable baseline. Exploitative play deliberately departs from it to attack an opponent's specific mistakes. Against weak players in soft games, exploitative play is "almost always more profitable," and using GTO against them is listed as a common mistake ([Card Player](https://www.cardplayer.com/online-poker/how-to-play-gto-poker)). For a beginner, that means learning GTO *concepts* such as ranges, position, sensible sizing and not folding too often, and using free solver-derived preflop charts, without memorising postflop solver output ([Card Player](https://www.cardplayer.com/online-poker/how-to-play-gto-poker); [Pokerfuse](https://pokerfuse.com/latest-news/2026/8/top-poker-training-tools-2026/)).

The most valuable exploit is how often players bluff big river bets. Upswing's database analysis found the player pool bluffing well below equilibrium at every heads-up river size, in pots under 20bb:

| River bet size | Population bluff share | GTO bluff share |
|---|---|---|
| 10–39% pot | 12% | about 18% |
| 40–59% pot | 16% | 25% |
| 60–89% pot | 19% | 30% |
| 90–110% pot | 22% | 33% |
| Overbets | 25% | about 37% |

Small and medium multiway river bets were even more value-heavy: 7% bluffs for block bets against about 18% at equilibrium. Upswing's conclusion is "unless you beat a significant amount of the opponent's value range, you should fold your hand" ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/3-cash-game-exploits-regs-backed-by-data/)). These are a vendor's own numbers, the article has internal inconsistencies, and it does not make clear which stakes the river data covers, so treat the gaps as direction rather than precise figures. The same pattern shows up elsewhere. Against typical, less aggressive opponents, Upswing has you fold about 47% to a flop check-raise instead of the solver's 34%, and re-raise about 1% instead of 6% ([Upswing](https://upswingpoker.com/vs-flop-check-raise/)). Against a raise from a nitty, passive player, "the only exploit… is to over-fold heavily," because your bluff-catcher's real equity is "closer to 0%" ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/the_3_biggest_leaks_killing_your_winrate/)). The other side of the same habit: players at these limits "tend to call too much," so river raises should be "clearly for value" ([BlackRain79](https://www.blackrain79.com/2020/12/raise-the-river.html)). Against passive opponents, "bet your strong hands yourself" instead of slowplaying to induce bluffs ([GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/the_3_biggest_leaks_killing_your_winrate/)). This is not universal. A low-reliability 2026 article claims some small-stakes players over-bluff rivers after a checked turn, and GTO Wizard has written about over-bluffed lines at lower limits ([DeucesCracked](https://www.deucescracked.com/blog/bluff-catching-small-stakes-online-poker-2026); [GTO Wizard](https://blog.gtowizard.com/calling-down-the-over-bluffed-lines-in-lower-limits/)). Aggregate under-bluffing does not mean every line is honest.

Labelling opponents makes these adjustments automatic. PokerListings' four standard types and their counters ([PokerListings](https://www.pokerlistings.com/blog/how-to-identify-and-exploit-different-poker-player-types)):

| Type | How they play | Your adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Tight-aggressive (TAG) | About 15–20% of hands; raises and re-raises strong hands, some well-timed bluffs | Avoid marginal spots and frequent bluffs; value bet relentlessly; respect their narrow range |
| Loose-aggressive (LAG) | 30%+ of hands; pressure on every street | Let them bluff: check-raise and call down with strong hands, re-raise strong hands, avoid marginal spots |
| Nit / tight-passive | Very narrow; rarely raises or bluffs; folds to pressure | Steal blinds, widen button opens, c-bet and raise to force folds; believe their strength |
| Calling station / loose-passive | 50%+ of hands; rarely folds or bluffs | Value bet big and often; never bluff |

Many beginners play like the calling station themselves ([PokerListings](https://www.pokerlistings.com/blog/how-to-identify-and-exploit-different-poker-player-types)). The common beginner leaks are well documented. They include playing too many hands (aim for **about 20% at 6-max and 15% at full ring**), and also playing too few, which lets others steal your blinds. They include slowplaying strong hands against passive players, ignoring position (steal with at least the top 30% or so from the cutoff and button), bluffing players who don't fold, judging results over days rather than months, and tilt ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/9-key-beginner-mistakes-low-stakes-how-to-fix-22957.htm)). BlackRain79's preflop list adds open-limping, calling out of position with dominated hands, and min-3-betting instead of re-raising to about 9bb in position or 12bb out of position against a 3bb open ([BlackRain79](https://www.blackrain79.com/2024/05/preflop-poker.html)). No source ranks these leaks by cost. A sensible order is to fix the free, every-hand leaks first: a position-based preflop chart, raising instead of limping, betting strong hands, and no bluffs against stations. Then add pot odds and folding to big aggression from passive players.

## A staged plan, a bankroll and a tilt routine protect the learning

None of the sources publishes a full curriculum, but they agree on the order and the method. Tendler advises mastering one concept before moving to the next ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/strategy-with-kristy-jared-tendler-the-mental-game-of-poker-11630.htm)). Tricia Cardner recommends a learn–apply–review cycle with one or two goals per session, a study journal, a weekly reflection, and roughly 70% practice to 30% theory ([Casino.org](https://www.casino.org/blog/the-ultimate-poker-study-guide/)). Pokerology recommends starting with cash games, which have fixed blinds, deep stacks and measurable results, before adding tournament concepts like ICM ([Pokerology](https://pokerology.com/lessons/cash-games-vs-tournaments/)). The table below is a staged plan built from those principles. The milestones are this report's suggestions, not a published standard.

| Stage | Focus | Milestone |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Preflop | Position charts, raising instead of limping, 3-bet sizes, blind play | Open, fold or 3-bet from any seat without hesitating; play about 20% of hands at 6-max |
| 2. Maths (alongside stage 1) | Pot odds, outs, implied odds, SPR | Work out the equity you need in a few seconds |
| 3. Postflop basics | C-betting by texture, value betting, no slowplaying against passive players | State why you bet (value or bluff) on every street in reviews |
| 4. Hand reading | Ranges, combinations, equity against ranges | Write down the opponent's likely range for each reviewed hand |
| 5. Opponent adjustments | Player types, notes, folding to passive aggression | Change at least one decision per session because of a read |
| 6. GTO concepts | Defence frequencies, polarised vs merged ranges, solver spot-checks | Explain why a solver mixes actions and when to deviate |
| 7. Optional: tournaments | Push/fold, ICM | Use free ICM calculators for stack decisions |

The free toolkit covers the first five stages. **Equilab** is a free equity calculator with an equity trainer, Windows only, with a $42/year Power version ([PokerListings](https://www.pokerlistings.com/poker-tools/calculators/equilab)). Upswing's **Preflop Prodigy** and the **Octopi Preflop Viewer** provide free solver-based charts, and PokerStove and the HoldemResources ICM calculator are free too ([Pokerfuse](https://pokerfuse.com/latest-news/2026/8/top-poker-training-tools-2026/)). **GTO Wizard's free plan** allows 10 trainer hands and one postflop solution a day ([VIP-Grinders](https://www.vip-grinders.com/poker-tools/gto-wizard/)), and paid tiers start at $49 a month ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/news/2026/03/gto-wizard-subscription-plans-new-features-pricing-50908.htm)). A tracker such as PokerTracker 4 costs $59.99 for small stakes and makes sense only once you play real online volume on a supported site; GGPoker is among those it does not support ([VIP-Grinders](https://www.vip-grinders.com/poker-tools/pokertracker-4-review/)). For reading, *Play Optimal Poker* (Andrew Brooks) is described as accessible to complete newcomers, *The Mental Game of Poker* (Tendler and Carter) is the standard mindset book, and *Modern Poker Theory* (Acevedo) is for later ([PokerListings](https://www.pokerlistings.com/blog/poker-books-and-videos)). Among paid training, a 2026 affiliate review names PokerCoaching.com, from $19 a month, as the best fit for beginners ([VIP-Grinders](https://www.vip-grinders.com/?p=145636)). The Red Chip Poker Podcast, built around one concept per episode, and Jonathan Little's Poker Coaching Podcast are free and beginner-friendly ([PokerListings](https://www.pokerlistings.com/blog/best-poker-podcasts)).

A bankroll is money set aside only for poker, and it keeps normal losing streaks from forcing you out of the game or onto tilt. 2026 guidance for no-limit cash games is **20 buy-ins (aggressive), 30 (standard) or 50 (conservative)**, and 100–200 buy-ins for tournaments. Move down once you fall below 20 buy-ins. Move up only when you have both the money and a proven win rate over **30,000–50,000 hands** ([VIP-Grinders](https://www.vip-grinders.com/bankroll-management/)). Newer players are advised to keep at least 40 buy-ins at micro stakes ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/9-key-beginner-mistakes-low-stakes-how-to-fix-22957.htm)). An older live-game guide from 2016 suggests 10–20 buy-ins, which is looser than current advice ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/how-to-manage-your-poker-bankroll-tips-and-recommendations-23914.htm)). For a student, the practical version is a separate budget you can afford to lose entirely. At 30–50 buy-ins, 2,000 NOK supports games with 40–65 NOK buy-ins.

The mental side is skill, not decoration. Tendler names **seven kinds of tilt**: running bad, injustice, hate-losing, mistake tilt, entitlement, revenge and desperation. Desperation is the urge to win losses back, and it drives long sessions and jumps in stakes. He treats tilt as a symptom of underlying flaws, and warns that "control is not a solution, it's a bandaid" and that "stopping tilt after it's been triggered is too late." Instead he recommends finding root causes with a written "mental hand history" ([PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/tilt-a-phenomenal-roi-killer-39932.htm)). His "inchworm" model says the most efficient improvement comes from raising your worst play, your C-game, not chasing peak performances ([US Chess](https://www.uschess.org/index.php/Jennifer-s-Blog/Chess-Lessons-From-the-Mental-Game-of-Poker.html)). Judge sessions by decision quality, not by the result, and quit when tilt appears ([Casino.org](https://www.casino.org/blog/the-ultimate-poker-study-guide/); [PokerNews](https://www.pokernews.com/strategy/9-key-beginner-mistakes-low-stakes-how-to-fix-22957.htm)). Live games add procedure that online software handles for you. Act in turn, avoid string bets (state a raise out loud or move all the chips in one motion), don't splash chips into the pot, and don't slow-roll a winning hand ([ThePokerBank](https://thepokerbank.com/articles/etiquette/live)).

For a player in Norway, where you can play legally limits the practice options. As of October 2026, **all legal poker in Norway is played in person**: private home games and the annual Norwegian Championship (NM) ([Regjeringen.no](https://www.regjeringen.no/contentassets/e36483a5672a41848338a296ea409198/horingsnotat-endringer-i-pengespillforskriften-nettpoker-m.m.-.pdf)). A secondary source summarises the home-game limits as no professional character, at most 20 participants, at most 1,000 NOK stake per player per gathering, and age 18+ ([ung.no](https://www.ung.no/oss/f0cossIsl6yKf8btL40WWW)). Check these against Lotteritilsynet before relying on them. NM 2026 was held at Gardermoen, where the 5,000 NOK Main Event drew a record 2,363 entries ([Gambling.com](https://www.gambling.com/no/news/poker-nm-2026-deltakerrekord-main-event)). On 29 September 2026 the government proposed letting Norsk Tipping offer online poker ([Regjeringen.no](https://www.regjeringen.no/no/aktuelt/foreslar-a-tillate-nettpoker-hos-norsk-tipping/id3173410/)). The proposal would allow cash games with blinds up to 5/10 NOK and a 1,000 NOK maximum per table, tournaments up to 2,500 NOK, and loss limits of 5,000 NOK a day and 10,000 NOK a month. Consultation closes on 29 November 2026, and no launch date has been set ([Regjeringen.no](https://www.regjeringen.no/contentassets/e36483a5672a41848338a296ea409198/horingsnotat-endringer-i-pengespillforskriften-nettpoker-m.m.-.pdf)). If adopted, the 1,000 NOK cap at a 10 NOK big blind means tables at most 100bb deep, exactly the depth the charts in this report assume. The research did not cover the legal position of Norwegians playing on foreign sites.

## Conclusion

The key change in thinking is that "how good is my hand?" has no fixed answer. The same top pair is a stack-off hand at SPR 3 and a pot-control hand at SPR 18. The same overpair is a monster on 8-7-3 and nearly dead on 8-5-4. The same bluff-catcher is a call against a station and a fold against a nit. Position, pot odds and SPR look like separate topics, but each measures one of two things: how much information you will have, and how much money one decision puts at risk. A beginner who checks those two things before every action is already past the stage of playing their cards in isolation.

The low-stakes edge also runs against intuition. Most profit comes from refusing to make losing plays: folding 70%+ of hands preflop, not paying off big river bets from passive players, and not bluffing people who don't fold. Fancy plays add little. The habit that loses money when you have it, calling too much, is the same one that pays you when opponents have it. That makes discipline the cheapest skill to learn and the most profitable at these stakes, which is why the preflop chart and the pot-odds table come before solver study. The uncertain parts, such as exact SPR cut-offs, population bluffing rates and the best small-blind strategy, matter much less than these fundamentals at the start.
